I.​
Introduction 
As part of the Climate Integrated Pathways for Podujeva (CLIPP) project, this competition aims 
to engage university students in solving realistic decision-making problems related to climate 
resilience using algorithmic methods. The primary objective of the first competition challenge is 
to expose participants to a computationally hard optimization problem that requires planning, 
scheduling, and heuristic reasoning under realistic constraints, while remaining solvable within a 
limited competition timeframe. 
To achieve this balance, we build upon the Street View Routing (SVR) [1] problem introduced 
in the inaugural edition of Google Hash Code, a team programming competition organized by 
Google and held annually from 2014 to 2023. Hash Code was explicitly designed to present 
participants with open-ended, real-world–inspired optimization problems for which exact 
solutions are infeasible at scale, encouraging the use of greedy methods, heuristics, and 
metaheuristics rather than closed-form algorithms or exhaustive search. 
II.​
Background: Google Hash Code and Street View Routing 
Google Hash Code was created by engineers at Google France as a university-oriented 
programming competition emphasizing teamwork, practical problem solving, and scalable 
algorithm design. From its first edition in 2014, Hash Code grew from a local event with a few 
hundred participants into a global competition with over 100,000 contestants, before being sunset 
in 2023 as part of Google’s broader Coding Competitions program. Throughout its history, Hash 
Code consistently focused on problems inspired by real engineering challenges faced at Google, 
particularly those involving large-scale optimization and trade-offs under constraints [2]. 
 

The Street View Routing problem was the very first problem used in the inaugural Hash Code 
competition in 2014. It was directly inspired by real optimization work at Google France related 
to planning routes for Street View cars capturing imagery of urban environments. The original 
problem models a city as a graph whose vertices represent street junctions and whose edges 
represent streets, which may be either one-directional or bidirectional. Each street is associated 
with a traversal time and a length, and all vehicles start from a common depot. 
 
Given a fixed time budget and a fleet of vehicles, the objective is to maximize the total length 
of unique streets traversed at least once by any vehicle. Traversing the same street multiple 
times, or in both directions, does not yield additional benefit. The problem was provided with 
multiple datasets of varying scale, ranging from small instances to large city-scale graphs, 
allowing participants to iteratively improve their solutions during the competition. 
The SVR problem strikes a careful balance between conceptual simplicity and algorithmic 
depth. While the input and output formats are straightforward, the combinatorial nature of the 
problem makes it highly challenging to optimize, especially for large instances. As a result, the 
problem is particularly well suited for academic competitions, where the emphasis is on 
reasoning, approximation, and solution quality rather than exact optimality. 
III.​
Computational Complexity and NP-Hardness 
From a computational perspective, the Street View Routing problem belongs to a broader family 
of graph traversal and coverage problems closely related to the postman problem. In particular, 
the version considered here can be viewed as a variant of the k-mixed postman problem. 

In the classical postman problem, the objective is to find a minimum-cost route that traverses 
every edge of a graph at least once. Several special cases are known to be tractable: the 
undirected postman problem and the directed postman problem (both for a single vehicle) admit 
polynomial-time solutions. However, when the graph contains a mixture of directed and 
undirected edges, the resulting mixed postman problem is known to be NP-hard. This hardness 
result is well established in the literature and has been explicitly discussed in analyses of the 
Street View Routing problem. 
The SVR problem generalizes this setting in multiple ways. First, the city graph is mixed, 
containing both one-directional and bidirectional streets. Second, instead of a single vehicle, the 
problem considers multiple vehicles operating simultaneously, each subject to its own time 
constraint. Finally, the objective is not to traverse all edges, but to select a subset of edges 
whose total length is maximized under a global time budget. This selective coverage 
objective further increases the combinatorial complexity of the problem. 
Because the mixed postman problem is NP-hard, and because Street View Routing strictly 
generalizes it, the SVR problem is also NP-hard. Consequently, computing optimal solutions for 
realistic instances is infeasible in practice, and effective approaches rely on heuristics, 
approximation strategies, and metaheuristics. This makes the problem an excellent vehicle for 
teaching and evaluating algorithmic thinking in the presence of computational intractability [3]. 
IV.​
Motivation for Use in the CLIPP Project 
We selected the Street View Routing problem as the foundation for this competition for several 
reasons: 
●​ Computational difficulty: The problem is NP-hard, naturally motivating heuristic and 
approximation-based approaches. 
●​ Competition-proven design: It was successfully used as the first problem in a 
large-scale international programming competition. 
●​ Clear optimization objective: The goal is unambiguous and easily measurable, enabling 
fair and transparent evaluation. 
●​ Academic suitability: The problem was originally designed for a university-focused 
competition and scales well to student-level time constraints. 
●​ Real-world relevance: The problem models realistic routing and coverage challenges 
encountered in urban infrastructure and logistics. 
 
V.​
Motivation for Adaptation to Climate Resilience 
While the original Street View Routing problem focuses purely on maximizing coverage 
efficiency, it does not explicitly account for environmental constraints or sustainability 
considerations. In the context of the Climate Integrated Pathways for Podujeva (CLIPP) project, 

whose objective is to explore algorithmic tools for climate-resilient municipal planning, we 
extend the original formulation to reflect resource-aware urban operations under real-world 
constraints. 
 
Water scarcity is a growing global challenge and is expected to increasingly affect municipalities 
worldwide, including the city of Podujeva. As climate change intensifies pressure on water 
resources, municipal services that depend on water—such as street cleaning and 
maintenance—must be planned with greater attention to resource efficiency. This motivates the 
introduction of routing and scheduling problems in which vehicle operations are constrained not 
only by time and distance, but also by limited and managed resources. 
To this end, we adopt the graph-based structure of the Street View Routing problem and 
introduce several modifications that adapt it to a realistic street-cleaning scenario. First, instead 
of a homogeneous fleet of identical vehicles, the problem features a heterogeneous fleet 
composed of three distinct vehicle types, each with different cleaning capabilities. Second, 
streets are no longer treated uniformly: they are classified into mandatory streets that must be 
serviced, optional streets that yield points when cleaned, and connector streets that serve only 
to enable movement through the network without contributing directly to the score. Third, unlike 
in the original SVR problem—where vehicles may end their routes at any junction—each vehicle 
is required to start from and return to a central depot within the allotted time, reflecting 
standard operational constraints of municipal service fleets. 

These extensions transform the problem from a purely coverage-based routing task into a 
planning and scheduling problem under resource and operational constraints, while 
preserving the combinatorial difficulty and heuristic nature of the original formulation. Through 
this adaptation, participants are challenged not only to design effective heuristics for an NP-hard 
optimization problem, but also to reason about trade-offs between operational efficiency, service 
prioritization, and resource conservation—skills that are increasingly central to climate-aware 
urban planning and decision-making.
