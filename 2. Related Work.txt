Related Work 
Methodology 
The literature review was conducted following a problem-driven approach. Starting from the 
practical problem of routing vehicles for street cleaning services, we identified that the 
underlying structure corresponds to an Arc Routing Problem (ARP). To understand the landscape 
of ARP variants, we first consulted key surveys and reviewed papers on arc routing [2]. From 
these reviews, we traced forward by examining the papers that cited them, as well as the 
references within them. We then narrowed our selection to studies whose problem variants share 
structural characteristics with ours — specifically, variants of the Rural Postman Problem (RPP) 
and its extensions involving mixed graphs, profit collection, time constraints, multiple vehicles, 
and heterogeneous fleets. This allowed us to systematically position our problem within the 
existing literature and identify the combination of features that distinguishes it from prior work. 
Literature review 
Literature on Arc Routing Problems (ARP) has been steadily growing, as many real-world 
applications can be formulated as an ARP [2]. These applications include street cleaning, salt 
spreading, snow clearing, street mapping, road maintenance, plotters, printers, cutting machines, 
newspaper delivery, postal services, and waste collection. 
At its core, our problem is an ARP: the city is represented as a graph where streets correspond to 
edges/arcs and junctions correspond to nodes, and vehicles must perform services along a subset 
of these edges. 
More specifically, our problem is a variant of the Rural Postman Problem (RPP) [3], one of the 
most well-studied special cases of the ARP. Unlike the Chinese Postman Problem (CPP) [19], 
which requires traversing all edges in a graph, the RPP requires traversing only a subset of them. 
Similarly, our problem distinguishes between required edges that must be serviced, non-required 
edges that serve only as connectors, and optional profitable edges that yield a score when visited. 
Since our graph contains both directed edges (one-way streets) and undirected edges (two-way 
streets), the problem falls under the Mixed Rural Postman Problem (MRPP) [4], a special case of 
the RPP defined on mixed graphs. However, our additional constraints — including vehicle 
heterogeneity, edge diversity, and time limitations — distinguish it from the standard MRPP. 
The presence of optional profitable edges relates our problem to the Prize-collecting Rural 
Postman Problem (PRPP) [5], in which there are no required edges and instead a profit is 
collected for each edge visited for the first time. Our variant differs in that it combines both 
required edges and profitable edges: in addition to the streets that must be serviced, there exists a 

set of optional edges from which a subset must be selected to maximize the total score collected. 
This aspect aligns with the broader class of Arc Routing Problems with Profits. 
Furthermore, our problem incorporates a hard time constraint, as each vehicle must complete its 
route and return to the depot within an eight-hour window. Time-constrained arc routing 
problems have been studied in the context of winter gritting operations [7], among others. 
Finally, our problem involves multiple vehicles with different capabilities. The use of more than 
one vehicle corresponds to the k-vehicle Arc Routing Problem (k-ARP) [1], [7], [8], [12], while 
the heterogeneity of the fleet — where vehicles differ in their operational characteristics — 
aligns with the Heterogeneous Fleet Arc Routing Problem (HARC) [14]. 
We have defined seven major features that define the structure of the ARP. Using these features 
we compare different variants of ARP to show that the new combinations of these features gives 
a new variant of the ARP for street cleaning. 
 
Features 
 
1.​ Required Visit​
The problem requires that only a subset of edges/arcs of the graph are visited. 
2.​ Mixed Graph​
The graph consists of both undirected edges and directed arcs. 
3.​ Time Constraint​
There is a strict limit on how long a vehicle can be out on the road. This includes both the 
time spent working (servicing a task) and the time spent driving between tasks. 
4.​ Multiple Vehicle​
You are managing a fleet of vehicles, not just a single driver. 
5.​ Depot Return​
Vehicles must start and end at the same location. 
6.​ Profit Collection​
Not all streets are equal; some have "prizes" or value attached to them. The algorithm 
focuses on collecting these values in addition that it has to visit the required edges. 
7.​ Heterogeneous Fleet​
The vehicles are not identical, they can differ in terms of capacity, speed and other 
characteristics.

Figure 1. Feature frequency distribution 
 
What we understand from Figure 1 is that the features are used in the previous problems, they are 
not unique to our study. The most studied and used features are the required visit of edges and 
the return to the depot of the vehicles. While the least studied and used features are the time 
constraints, profit collection and the heterogenous fleet. Our study uses a combination of all 
these features making it unique accoros the different ARP variants.
