Scope and Purpose--The problem of routing vehicles arises very frequently in practice. The difficulty of 
the problem and its solution approaches are very sensitive to the application and the characteristics of 
the travel network. In this paper, we present a solution approach for the problem of routing a single 
vehicle over a mixed and disconnected network. A number of real life vehicle routing applications in which 
travel must be made on some specified streets belong to this class of problem. Some examples of such 
problems are: waste collection from residential areas, street sweeping and rural postman problem. The 
designed solution procedures are fast and are implemented on a personal computer. 
Abstract--This study addresses the problem of routing vehicles over the specified segments of a mixed 
network. The routing problem is modeled as a Capacitated General Routing Problem (CGRP). The 
solution to the problem is a set of routes for the vehicles which are consistent with the capacity and 
work-hours constraints. For the CGRP, we present a heuristic procedure to solve for large size problems. 
Both route-first-partition-next (RFPN) and partition-first-and-route-next (PFRN) approaches are 
discussed. Computational results of the developed procedure are also presented. 
1. INTRODUCTION 
This paper addresses the problem of routing vehicles over the specified segments of a mixed network. 
Such problems arise very frequently in real-life applications of waste collection [1], street sweeping 
[2], routing of sanitation vehicles [3], routing of electric meter readers [4], school bus routing [5] 
and other problems of this nature. 
The routing problem in which travel is done over the streets of a network such that each street 
is covered at least once and the total distance traveled is minimized is known as the Chinese 
Postman Problem (CPP). The other arc-routing problems are variants of the CPP. For example, 
if there is a limit on the vehicle capacity or shift time then the resulting problem is called the 
Capacitated Chinese Postman Problem (CCPP). Sometimes it is required that only some specified 
street segments of the network need be visited. Then the routing problem is called the Rural 
Postman Problem (RPP). However, if routing is to be done on some specified street segments and 
some specified nodes then the problem is called the General Routing Problem (GRP). In the RPP 
and the GRP if the capacity and time restrictions are explicitly considered then the resulting 
problems are called the Capacitated RPP (CRPP) and the Capacitated GRP (CGRP), respectively. 
In the problem addressed in this study, the vehicles are required to travel on specified street segments 
and specified nodes. Therefore, we model this routing problem as a CGRP. Classification of different 
vehicle routine and scheduling problems and their solution strategies are discussed in Refs [6-9]. 
There are a number of software systems in practice today which address various applications of 
the capacitated routing problem. A survey and comparison of these software packages are given 
in Refs [10, 11].

The CPP and its variants are well studied in the literature. The solution procedures to CPP are 
sensitive to the nature of travel network. Edmonds and Johnson [12] give polynomial algorithms 
for CPP when the travel network is completely directed or undirected. However, the problem 
becomes difficult to solve (NP-complete) if the travel network contains both the directed and 
undirected arcs. Such a travel network is called a mixed network. Frederickson [13, 14] and Minieka 
[15] study the CPP on a mixed network and give approximate solution procedures for this problem. 
Since the CPP is a relaxed form of the CCPP (obtained by ignoring capacity and time restrictions), 
an optimal solution to the CPP forms a lower bound to the CCPP. 
In practice, solutions to large CCPP are obtained using heuristic solution procedures. These 
procedures fall into two broad classes: (i) route-first-partition-next (RFPN) and (ii) partition-first- 
and-route-next (PFRN). The RFPN procedures first form a large CPP tour by ignoring the 
restrictions on the vehicle capacity and tour length and then partition this large tour into smaller 
vehicle routes consistent with the restrictions on vehicle capacity and route length. One limitation 
of this procedure is to control the regional overlapping of the routes which may be undesirable 
from an administrative point of view. To overcome this, the PFRN approach is used. The PFRN 
approach first partitions the large region into smaller zones and then routing is performed for 
each zone separately. This method reduces the interweaving of routes, however the overall solution 
value depends upon the partitioning of the region which is done subjectively. 
Bodin et al. [1-3] solve the CCPP in which all arcs are directed using a slightly modified 
Edmonds and Johnson's algorithm to solve the CPP. They discuss both the RFPN and the PFRN 
approaches. 
Procedures which build tours by successive augmentation of arcs are studied by Golden et al. 
[16, 17]. They compare the performance of two heuristics for the CCPP, namely, Golden and 
Wong's Augment-Merge heuristic and the Construct-Strike algorithm by Christofides with their 
Path Scanning algorithm for the CCPP on an undirected connected network. The Augment-Merge 
and Path Scanning algorithms perform better than Construct-Strike algorithm when compared 
with the lower bound. The Augment-Merge takes more computation time when compared with 
the other two algorithms. 
Wen-Lea Pearn [18] compares various algorithms for solving the CCPP: Construct-Strike 
algorithm by Christofides [19], the Path-Scanning and Augment-Merge algorithms by Golden et 
al. [16], the Modified Construct-Strike and Random Path-Scanning algorithms by Pearn [20-1, and 
the Augment-Insert Algorithm by Pearn [18]. He shows that Augment-Insert performs better than 
other algorithms when compared with the lower bound. The computation time for the 
Augment-Insert algorithm is considerably less than the Modified Construct-Strike algorithm, 
Construct-Strike algorithm and Augment-Merge Algorithms. 
The GRP is studied by Orloff [21,22], and Lenstra and Rinnooy Kan [23]. However, these 
studies do not consider the restrictions on vehicle capacities and available time. 
In the case of graphs with disconnected components the augmentatioon procedures work best 
when the arcs are directed. This is because there is a unique way to travel from one arc to another. 
In the case of a mixed network or when the arcs are undirected, these procedures are not easy. 
This is due to the large number of possible ways to combine arcs. Therefore, for mixed graphs with 
disconnected components other solution strategies need to be examined. In this paper we present 
a heuristic approach which develops routes for the vehicles when the set of edges to be traveled 
form a mixed and disconnected graph. 
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. The overall problem is formally described 
in Section 2. The solution approach developed is explained in detail in Section 3. Section 4 presents 
lower bounds and computational results. Section 5 concludes the paper with a summary. 
Descriptions of the procedures used in the solution algorithm are included in Appendix I.

We consider the problem of routing vehicles over the streets of residential areas and to convenience 
stores located within these areas. The streets used for traveling define a travel network. In this 
network, street segments allowing two-way (one-way) travel form edges (arcs) and the points of 
intersection of street segments define nodes. Furthermore, locations of convenience stores are also

In this network, since we need to visit both the specified edges (arcs) and the nodes, the problem

3. SOLUTION PROCEDURES 
In our solution procedure to CGRP, we first condense the large travel network by considering 
only those arcs and nodes from which collection is to be made. The condensed network is generally 
a disconnected network with a number of small components. Since, for tours to be feasible, there 
should be a path between every two nodes, we add cheapest (minimum cost) edges and arcs to this 
condensed graph such that the condensed graph is strongly connected. To generate individual 
vehicle routes, we use a RFPN approach. First, a CPP tour is formed on the strongly connected 
graph. In this tour it is possible that some edges (arcs) are traveled more than once. If we explicitly 
augment the graph with the extra copies of these edges (arcs) equal to the number of times they 
are visited, the resulting graph becomes Eulerian. The Euler tour covers all the specified edges 
(arcs) and nodes. This Euler tour is then partitioned into individual vehicle routes. 
As discussed earlier, this procedure when applied to the overall region (a city) may create 
interweaving routes which are difficult to manage administratively. However, in practice the overall 
region is generally partitioned into service zones (suburbs) and service is provided to each zone 
individually. Moreover, not all zones are serviced on the same day. The solution approach described 
below can be used for each zone individually; thereby reducing the regional overlapping of the routes 
and the management related problems. 
3.1. Procedure to Solve CGRP 
Golden and Wong [24] show that even the 0.5 approximate CCPP which attempts to find a 
solution with a cost less than 1.5 times the value of the optimal solution is NP-hard. Since CCPP 
is a relaxation of the CGRP, the 0.5 approximate CGRP is also NP-hard. Therefore, approximate 
solution procedures are developed to solve the large-size real-life problems. The algorithm ROUTE1 
solves CGRP on mixed networks and is explained below.

The procedure starts with the condensed sub-graph of the travel network in which only those 
arcs, edges and nodes are considered which needs to be visited. Since the condensed sub-graph is 
generally disconnected, the procedure first augments the sub-graph with cheapest arcs and edges 
such that the subgraph becomes strongly connected. Finally, the strongly connected sub-graph is 
converted into an Eulerian graph which admits a tour. Following each step of the ROUTE1 
algorithm is explained in detail. Descriptions of the algorithms used are given in Appendix I. 
Step 1. The algorithm EXTRACT extracts the arcs, edges and nodes that need to be visited 
from the initial graph Go and forms a condensed graph, say G1 =(V1, R1). The vertex set I/1 contains 
the set V o and the vertices associated with links in {Ao u Eo}. The set R~ = {Ao u Eo}. The condensed 
graph is gererally a disconnected graph. 
Step 2. The disconnected components of G1 are joined together by finding a Minimum Spanning 
Tree (MST) between these components. To find a MST, we first form a complete graph H=(W, E) 
from graph G~, where W is the set of vertices and E is the edge set. The vertices of set W are formed 
by contracting each disconnected component as a composite node. Thus, the number of vertices 
in W are equal to the number of disconnected components in G~. An edge (u, v) in the set E is a 
composite edge corresponding to the shortest path in Go connecting components u and v of graph 
G1. It is possible that a shortest path in graph Go may contain both arcs and edges. If a shortest 
path contains arcs then it is represented as a composite arc oriented in the direction of the arcs 
on this path. If a shortest path contains only edges, it is represented as an composite edge. In edge 
set E all composite arcs are initially considered as composite edges by temporarily ignoring their 
direction. 
A MST is formed in graph H. Once the MST is formed, the direction of arcs are restored and 
graph G~ is augmented with the links of MST. The composite nodes are expanded back to their 
original forms. Algorithm MINSPA finds the MST connecting the disconnected components as in 
Ref. [25]. Let G2 =(1/2, R2) be the connected graph after Step 2. The vertex set I/2 = 1/1 and R2 = {R~ 
and the composite links added due to MST}. 
After restoration of direction on arcs in the connected graph it is possible that some nodes have 
zero in-degree or zero out-degree. To ensure that such nodes are reachable from other nodes 
and vice-versa, we make the graph G 2 strongly connected. This is accomplished as follows. 
Step 3. A strongly connected component (SCC) of G2 is a subgraph of G2 in which for every 
pair of vertices u and v there exists a path from u to v and a path from v to u. Tarjan [26] gives 
an algorithm, which is polynomial in the number of nodes and arcs of the graph, to identify all its 
SCCs. However, the general problem of finding a SCC such that the component is of minimum 
weight remains NP-Complete. Eswaran and Tarjan [27] show that a special case of this problem 
in which all weights are equal is solvable in linear time with respect to the number of arcs. 
To make G2 a strongly connected graph, we need to add additional links to G2 such that there 
is path between every pair of vertices in G2. Moreover since the objective is to minimize the total 
distance traveled, this augmentation should be such that the sum of the lengths of these links is a 
minimum. The problem of selecting additional links is equivalent to the problem of finding minimum 
weight strongly connected components of a graph. We present a fast heuristic to solve this problem.

4.1. Computational Results 
The developed algorithms, which are outlined in Appendix I, were coded in FORTRAN 77. The 
size of the problem that can be solved using these algorithms depends on the following factors:

the arcs in the network are directed respectively. As the percentage of arcs directed in the network 
increases, the ratio of the solution to the lower bound increases. This may be attributed to a weak 
lower bound. The lower bound proposed by Golden and Wong is for an undirected network. For 
test problems with 60% of the directed arcs, this bound results in a weaker lower bound with a

because as the percentage of directed arcs increases, the number of components in the graph increases.

directed the CPU time is the least. The solution value also increases as the number of directed 
arcs increases because the distance to reach some of the nodes or arcs from the depot increases as 
the arcs are directed. 
The solution value decreases as the percentage of the arcs to be visited are decreased because 
relatively less travel time is required as shown in Tables 5-8. The CPU time first decreases with 
the decrease in the number of arcs to be visited due to smaller problem size. However, as the 
number of arcs to be visited is decreased further, the number of disconnected components in the 
resulting condensed graph increases. Consequently, more CPU time is required to make the graph 
strongly connected. 
5. SUMMARY 
In this study, the problem of routing of vehicles over the specified segments of a mixed network 
is addressed. The problem is modeled as a CGRP. Since this problem is NP-hard, a heuristic 
procedure is developed to solve this problem. The problem of finding minimum weight strongly 
connected components of a graph arises as a sub-problem. This is a NP-hard problem for its general 
case. We present a heuristic procedure to solve it. 
The developed software is packaged as a micro-computer based interactive optimization system. 
The software is user friendly, detects input data errors, provides help and is interactive. The user 
can also evaluate his/her own routes against those generated by the software to compare the 
alternate routing strategies. The overall system can be used for planning, cost estimation or for 
daily operations.
