This article introduces the Capacitated Arc Routing with In-
termediate Facilities (CARPIF), a variant of the classical
Capacitated Arc Routing Problem (CARP) in which the vehi-
cle may unload or replenish at intermediate facilities.
Two lower bounds are developed for the CARPIF: The
ﬁrst is based on the Rural Postman Problem (RPP) and
the second one uses a relaxation of an integer lin-
ear formulation of the problem. Two upper bounds are
also developed, based on the solution of an RPP and
of a CARP. Computational results on a set of bench-
mark instances conﬁrm the quality of the proposed
bounds. © 2001 John Wiley & Sons, Inc.

1. INTRODUCTION
The purpose of this article was to develop lower bounds
and heuristics for the Capacitated Arc Routing Prob-
lem with Intermediate Facilities (CARPIF), a variant of
the classical Capacitated Arc Routing Problem (CARP)
(see, e.g., Golden and Wong [16], Eiselt et al. [10, 11],
Assad and Golden [1]). Both these problems, in their
undirected version, are deﬁned on a graph G = (V, E),
where V = {v1, . . . , vn} is a vertex set including a de-
pot v1 and E is a set of edges (vi, vj)(i < j), includ-
ing a subset R of required edges. Each edge of R must
be serviced once but can be traversed several times.

If e /∈R, then qe = 0. Denote by dij the length of a
shortest chain between two vertices vi and vj. In the
CARP, one must determine a least-cost traversal, or tour,
of all edges of R such that the total demand of any chain
(vi1 = v1, vi2, . . . , vit = v1), where vi2, . . . , vit−1 ≠v1, does
not exceed a given vehicle capacity Q. In the CARPIF,
the set V contains a subset I of intermediate facilities
(IFs), possibly including v1. The aim is to determine a
least-cost tour (vi1 = v1, vi2, . . . , vit = v1) of all edges of
R such that the total demand of any chain (vi1, . . . , vir),
where vir ∈I, r ≠t, vi2, . . . , vir−1 /∈I, or of any chain
(vir, . . . , vis), where vir, vis ∈I, vir+1, . . . , vis−1 /∈I, does not
exceed Q. In other words, starting from the depot, the ve-
hicle traverses edges of E, collects demands on required
edges, and visits IFs to unload. The total demand accu-
mulated between the depot and the ﬁrst IF or between
two successive IFs may never exceed Q. If the last IF on
the tour is not the depot, then the ﬁnal chain between that
facility and the depot does not have any positive demand
and is therefore unconstrained (see Fig. 1). This article
can be viewed as a ﬁrst step toward solving more gen-
eral problems involving several vehicles, as is often the
case in practice. Our results can be used to generate good
solutions to multivehicle problems by ﬁrst assigning re-
quired edges to individual vehicles and then working on
each route separately.
A diﬀerent but conceptually equivalent version of the
CARPIF is where the vehicle makes deliveries instead
of collections. Then, it must replenish at IFs in order to

The CARPIF is NP-hard since it reduces to the CARP,
another NP-hard problem [15], whenever I = {v1}. In ad-
dition, if {v1} ⊂I, then the CARPIF is a relaxation of
the CARP since any CARP solution is feasible for the
CARPIF. If I = {vr} but r ≠1, then the correspond-
ing CARPIF can easily be viewed as a CARP with a
depot located at vr provided that an artiﬁcial required
edge (v1, vr) is introduced with q1r = 0 and c1r = d1r.

can sometimes be used to remove some dominated IFs:

containing f, and this facility can therefore be discarded.
The remainder of this article is organized as follows:
In Section 2, we develop a lower-bounding procedure for
the CARPIF based on the formulation of one of its relax-
ations. Two heuristics are presented in Section 3. Com-
putational results are provided in Section 4, followed by
conclusions in Section 5.
2. LOWER BOUNDS
In this section, we develop two lower bounds for the
CARPIF. The ﬁrst is based on the Rural Postman Prob-
lem (RPP), a natural relaxation of the CARPIF. The sec-
ond uses an integer linear formulation of the problem
with deadheading variables.
2.1.
The RPP Lower Bound
First, observe that the CARP solution does not pro-
vide a valid lower bound on the value of the correspond-
ing CARPIF. To see this, consider the graph depicted in

However, the RPP (which can be deﬁned as a CARP
with Q = ∞) provides a natural lower bound for the
CARPIF. If z∗

sociated with a given graph, then z∗

CARPIF. Since
the RPP is itself NP-hard, it may not be practical solve
this problem optimally. Instead, one can use an easy-to-
compute lower bound zRPP on z∗

erated by means of a branch-and-cut procedure. Thus,
using an integer linear programming formulation and ad-
ditional valid inequalities, Ghiani and Laporte [14] were
able to obtain zRPP values at the root of the branch-and-
cut tree, varying between 0.987 and 0.999 of z∗
RPP, on
randomly generated graphs containing between 50 and
350 vertices, within very short computing times. While
it would seem natural to use an RPP lower bound, this
bound can sometimes be quite weak since it ignores ca-
pacity constraints and the location of IFs. For this reason,
we have developed an alternative bound.

with a workable integer linear programming formula-
tion of the CARPIF that does not contain too many vari-
ables and constraints. However, a good relaxation can be
formulated in terms of so-called deadheading variables.

the spirit of the Belenguer and Benavent [3, 4] formu-

edge between S and its complement.

3. HEURISTICS
We have developed two heuristics for the CARP. The
ﬁrst computes an RPP solution on G and derives from
it a feasible CARPIF solution by optimally introducing
IFs by means of a shortest path algorithm. The second
applies a CARP heuristic to a transformed graph, makes
the solution feasible for the CARPIF, and ﬁnally applies
an improvement heuristic.
3.1.
RPP-based Heuristic
In the RPP-based heuristic, edge demands are ﬁrst
ignored and an RPP feasible solution is determined by
means of Frederickson’s heuristic [13], which ﬁrst con-
structs a shortest tree spanning all components of re-
quired edges and then computes an optimal matching
of odd-degree vertices. In a second stage, demands are
reintroduced and IFs are optimally inserted for each ori-
entation of the RPP solution by solving a shortest path
problem on an auxiliary directed graph, in the spirit of
Beasley’s [2] route-ﬁrst/cluster-second heuristic for the
capacitated Vehicle Routing Problem and of Jansen’s [19]
heuristic for the CARP. More formally, the heuristic can
be described as follows:
Step 1. Apply Frederickson’s RPP heuristic on G.

CARP-based Heuristic
We have also devised a second heuristic in which
a CARP is ﬁrst solved on a modiﬁed graph G′. If the
solution is feasible for the CARPIF, the process ends.
Otherwise, a feasible CARPIF solution is obtained by
solving an RPP on an auxiliary multigraph H, and an
improvement is sought in a third and ﬁnal step. These
three steps are now described:

and E′

E′\E, a zero cost to all (v0, f) edges (f ∈I), and deﬁne
c0,1 = minf∈I{d1f}. Solve a CARP on G′ with required
edge set R′ and a depot located at v0. Delete v0 and all
edges of E′\E and replace edge (v0, v1) by a shortest
chain between v1 and vf∗, where f∗= arg minf∈I{d1f}.
If the graph resulting from the CARP solution and the
subsequent edge deletions is Eulerian, it then constitutes
a feasible CARPIF solution since it can be decomposed
into a connected set of routes of demand not exceeding
Q, all starting and ending at an IF, and one of these routes
contains a zero-demand chain between v1 and f∗∈I.
The procedure then terminates. In our implementation,
the CARP is solved heuristically using the CARPET al-
gorithm of Hertz et al. [17]. If the graph is not Eulerian,
it corresponds to an infeasible CARPIF solution. The
algorithm proceeds to Step 2 in order to make the re-
sulting graph Eulerian. The feasible and infeasible cases
are depicted in Figures 3 and 4.
Step 2. Deﬁne an auxiliary undirected multigraph H =
(W, F). The vertex set W is the subset of IFs selected by
the CARP algorithm in Step 1. The edge set F contains
one required edge for each IF-to-IF chain in the CARP
solution translated on G, as well as one nonrequired edge
corresponding to each shortest chain on G between any
pair of two diﬀerent vertices of W. Assign to each re-
quired edge the value of its corresponding cost in G and
to each nonrequired edge a cost equal to the length of
the corresponding shortest chain in G. Solve the RPP
on H and translate the solution into a CARPIF solution
on G (the above construction process guarantees that it
is always feasible). In our implementation, the RPP is
solved heuristically by ﬁrst applying Frederickson’s [13]
heuristic and then the SHORTEN procedure developed
by Hertz et al. [18] for the RPP.

The results presented in Tables 2 and 6 show that
UB2 is almost always better than is UB1, but this comes
at the expense of much larger computation times. The
average percentage deviation of the best upper bound
over the best lower bound is 1.25 for the DeArmon in-
stances (maximum value: 5.70), 2.78 for the Benavent
instances (maximum value: 11.14), and 0.81 for the
Hertz–Laporte–Mittaz instances (maximum value: 3.80).
This conﬁrms the quality of both our lower- and upper-
bounding procedures.
5. CONCLUSIONS
We have introduced the CARPIF, a variant of the CARP
in which the vehicle may unload or replenish at IFs. Ap-
plications of the CARPIF are encountered, for example,
in garbage collection and road gritting. We have inves-
tigated several properties of the CARPIF and provided
lower- and upper-bounding procedures. Computational
results on three sets of instances conﬁrm the quality of
both bounds.
