Abstract
The Rural Postman Problem on a mixed graph (MRPP) consists of "nding a minimum cost tour which
traverses, at least once, the arcs and edges of a given subset of the arcs and edges of the graph. This problem is
known to be NP-hard. This paper presents two heuristic approaches to solve it. An approximate algorithm
based on the resolution of some #ow and matching problems and a tabu search implementation is presented.
The tabu search algorithm seeks high-quality tours by means of a switching mechanism in an intensi"cation
phase and two levels of diversi"cation. Computational results are presented to assess the merits of the
method.
Scope and purpose
Routing Problems arise in several areas of distribution management and logistics and their practical
signi"cance has been well documented in the literature. Arc Routing Problems are routing problems where
a single vehicle or a #eet of vehicles must service the arcs of a transportation network. Such problems occur in
a variety of practical contexts, such as garbage collection, mail delivery, snow removal and school bus
routing. In this paper we focus on the Mixed Rural Postman Problem (MRPP), an NP-hard problem that
includes, as special cases, the well-known Chinese Postman Problem and the Rural Postman Problem. The
MRPP "ts real-world situations more closely than other simpler problems. In this paper we present heuristic
procedures for the MRPP, where the e!ectiveness of the procedures is measured by the combination of
solution quality and speed. ( 2000 Elsevier Science Ltd. All rights reserved.

1. Introduction
Given a connected undirected graph or multigraph G"(<, E), with a set of vertices <, a set of
edges E and a nonnegative cost vector associated with E, the celebrated Chinese Postman Problem
(CPP) is that of "nding a minimum-cost closed walk traversing each edge of E at least once. This
problem, as well as its directed version, the CPP de"ned on a directed graph (DCPP), is solvable in
polynomial time [1], whereas the corresponding problem de"ned on a mixed graph is known to be
NP-hard [2]. In the Mixed Chinese Postman Problem (MCPP) the edges of the graph (two way
streets) may be traversed in both directions with the same cost, while the arcs (one way streets) have
to be traversed in only one speci"ed direction. This interesting extension of the CPP re#ects
real-life situations more directly. Heuristic algorithms for the MCPP have been proposed by
Frederickson [3] and Pearn and Liu [4]. GroK tschel and Win [5] and Nobert and Picard [6]
devised LP-based cutting-plane algorithms solving to optimality instances of medium sizes.
On the other hand, another more practical generalization of the CPP is the Rural Postman
Problem (RPP), in which only a subset, ER, of the edges in E are required to be traversed at
minimum cost. The RPP and its directed version, the Directed Rural Postman Problem (DRPP),
are both NP-hard [7]. Recent heuristic procedures for the RPP have been proposed by Pearn and
Wu [8] and Hertz et al. [9], while its associated polyhedron is studied in CorberaH n and Sanchis
[10,11] and Letchford [12].
All the above-mentioned problems are included in the class of Arc Routing Problems, those
routing problems where a vehicle or a #eet of vehicles must service the `arcsa of a transportation
network for the purpose of garbage collection, mail delivery, snow removal, inspection of electric
power lines or oil or gas pipelines, street sweepers, meter reading, spraying roads with salt and
school bus routing, and have been the object of great attention by operations researchers. Excellent
surveys on the area are those recently published by Eiselt et al. [13,14] and Assad and Golden [15].
In this paper we focus on the RPP de"ned on a mixed graph, the Mixed Rural Postman Problem
(MRPP), a problem including as special cases all those mentioned above, and that accommodates
real-world situations more closely. In fact, some of us faced this problem while designing a code for
the garbage collection in medium size cities [16].
The MRPP may be de"ned as follows. Let G"(<, E, A) be a strongly connected mixed graph,
where <"M1, 2,2, nN is the vertex set and E and A are the set of edges and arcs, respectively. The
arcs or edges of G will be termed the links of the graph. A nonnegative cost cij is associated with
every link (i, j). Let ER-E and AR-A be the set of (required) edges and (required) arcs,
respectively, that must be serviced. Then, the MRPP is to "nd a minimum cost closed walk (tour)
traversing each required link (edge or arc) at least once. Note that if (i, j) is an edge of cost ce, then
the cost of traversing (i, j) in either direction is ce, while if (i, j) is an arc of cost ce, then the cost of
traversing (i, j) is ce if the route goes from i to j, and R otherwise. Obviously, the MCPP can be
derived from the MRPP for ER"E and AR"A, while the RPP is derived from the MRPP for
A"0. Then, the MRPP is a generalization of two NP-hard problems and, hence, is itself NP-hard.
In this paper we present two heuristic algorithms for the MRPP: a constructive algorithm to
obtain good solutions in short computation time (Section 3), and a local search procedure that
seeks high-quality solutions (Section 5). The "rst one is partially based on the algorithm for
the MCPP proposed in [17]. The second one uses di!erent tabu search elements, such as
memory structures, attractiveness based on in#uence and probabilistic strategies to overcome local

5. Tabu search algorithm
Tabu Search (TS) is an adaptive technique for solving hard combinatorial optimization prob-
lems based on general principles of intelligent problem solving. In essence it is a metaheuristic,
which may be used to guide any local search procedure in the aggressive search for a global
optimum, using memory structures to avoid getting trapped in local optima. A complete and
precise description of TS may be found in [20]. The following subsections describe the elements
that are critical in the development of the tabu search procedure for the Mixed Rural Postman
Problem.
Our procedure starts from a MRPP tour obtained with the constructive algorithm. It is
represented by a completely directed and strongly connected graph, containing all the required
links (a direction has been assigned to original edges) and satisfying the symmetry condition (the
number of arcs entering and leaving each vertex is the same). As during the search process
disconnected `toursa (i.e., symmetric graphs consisting of several subtours) will be allowed, we will
also use the term solution to designate them, for the sake of simplicity.
5.1. Neighborhood dexnition
Switches are used as the primary mechanism in our TS scheme to move from one solution to
another. Let (i, j) be a connecting arc and let us denote by [k, i] and [ j, s] paths from k to i and
from j to s, respectively, in the augmentation of the graph induced by the required links. We de"ne
move m(i, j) as deleting path [[k, i], (i, j), [ j, s]] from the current solution, and adding the shortest
path from k to s. Fig. 9 illustrates this move.
Each move has an associated value given by the di!erence between the objective function values
before and after the move. It can be computed as the cost of the deleted path [[k, i], (i, j), [ j, s]]
minus the cost of the added shortest path.

5.2. Basic procedure
Starting from a solution obtained with the constructive algorithm, the basic TS procedure
alternates between an intensi"cation and a diversi"cation phase as described below.
5.2.1. Intensixcation phase
An iteration of this phase begins by randomly selecting a connecting arc. The probability of
selecting arc (i, j) is proportional to its cost. Move m(i, j) is executed and the removed arc (i, j)
becomes tabu-active for tabu tenure iterations, and therefore it cannot be added to the solution
during this time.
In subsequent iterations, a connecting arc is randomly selected as before. Its corresponding move
is performed if it is available and does not add any tabu arc. Since a shortest path replaces a path
from k to s, the objective function decreases (or keeps its value). The intensi"cation phase ends
when no connecting arc with an associated available move exists.
5.2.2. Diversixcation phase
This phase is performed for maxiter iterations. In each iteration, a nontabu connecting arc (i, j) is
probabilistically selected as in the intensi"cation phase. If move m(i, j) is available and does not add
a tabu arc, it is performed. Otherwise, two alternative moves are considered, depending on whether
the current solution is connected or not.
If the current solution is connected, the shortest path to be added in the move m(i, j) is computed
in the graph obtained by deleting the tabu arcs. Therefore, this restricted move may, or may not,
improve the objective function value. The move is executed even when its value is not positive,
resulting in a deterioration of the current objective function value.
If the current solution is nonconnected, the algorithm performs the following move in order to
connect two components. It randomly selects a connected component and considers all connecting
arcs (u, v) from this component in which the shortest paths from v to u do not include a tabu arc.
The lowest cost one, considering the arc plus the shortest return path costs, is selected. If all the
above paths include a tabu arc, we ignore the tabu status (aspiration criteria by default). This
connecting move obviously increases the objective function value, but connects two components.
It is worth mentioning that the interplay among these moves keeps a high degree of connectivity
in the solution, which is enough to obtain a large number of feasible solutions during the search
process.
Both phases are alternated until the global stopping criterion is met. The procedure "nishes
when, after a number of iterations equal to nonimprove, none of the visited solutions improves the
best known one.

were programmed in C and compiled in Microsoft Visual C##5.0. The instances were generated
according to the following parameters: number of R-connected components (p), number of vertices
(D<D), number of arcs (DAD), number of required arcs (DARD), number of edges (DED) and number of
required edges (DERD).
The graph generator works as follows: vertices are equally distributed among components. Each
component has xD<D/py vertices, except possibly the last one which also has the di!erence up to D<D.
Arcs and edges are generated, up to a pre"xed number, by randomly selecting two vertices and
adding the corresponding link.
To generate required links, if the two vertices do not belong to the same component they are
rejected and a new selection is made. The "nal number of nonrequired arcs might be larger, in some
instances, than the pre"xed number DACARD, in order to obtain a strongly connected graph.
This generator allows up to four links between two vertices: one for each kind of link (required
and nonrequired arc, required and nonrequired edge). We have randomly generated 100 instances
with the following ranges:
p3[3, 12],
D<D3[20, 100],
DED3[15, 220],
DERD3[5, 150],
DAD3[50, 350],
DARD3[5, 200]
The cost of each link has been randomly generated in the range 1}20. These ranges have been
chosen in a way similar to that of other arc routing works [8]. Table 7 in the Appendix shows the
parameters of each instance.
Preliminary tests, on some of the biggest instances from Table 7 (those with extension `c10a),
were performed in order to determine the values of the key parameters in our search scheme. We
found that Pt"1.3 and Global"10 produced the best results in terms of solution quality and
CPU time. Nevertheless, in the case of nonimprove and maxiter the results were not so conclusive.
Therefore, we have reported seven variants of the TS procedure according to di!erent values of
nonimprove and maxiter. Mixed 1 provides a combination of the tested parameters' values depend-
ing on the number p of R-connected components of the instances, as follows: if p)5, then
nonimprove"50 and maxiter"50; if 6)p)10, then nonimprove"40 and maxiter"75; "nally,
if 11)p, then nonimprove"25 and maxiter"100. In order to see the in#uence of Global, Mixed
2 provides the same combination as Mixed 1 but with Global equal to 15. We have also reported
the results of the algorithm described in Section 3, which will be denoted by Constructive.
As far as we know, no other heuristic or exact solution method for the MRPP has been
published. Then, in order to measure the quality of the solutions obtained by the heuristics
described above, we have computed a lower bound to the optimal solution cost. This bound has
been obtained with an LP-based cutting plane algorithm from the partial description of the MRPP
polyhedron given in [21]. In this study, it is shown that connectivity, R-odd and balanced set
inequalities, among others, de"ne facets of the MRPP polyhedron. Therefore, they are among the
best cutting planes that can be found. Furthermore, separation problems associated to connectivity
and R-odd constraints are solvable in polynomial time by means of max-#ow calculations and the
Padberg and Rao [22] method to "nd minimum odd cut-sets. Nobert and Picard [6] show that

identifying violated balanced constraints for the MCPP can also be solved in polynomial time. This
result is easily extended to the MRPP.
Our cutting plane procedure starts with a linear program (LP) including the non-negative
constraints, connectivity constraints corresponding to the p R-connected components, R-odd
inequalities associated to R-odd vertices (those incident with an odd number of required links) and
balanced set constraints associated to unbalanced vertices. In the course of the algorithm,
additional inequalities of the above-mentioned classes are generated as they are found to be
violated. The procedure stops when this is no longer possible. The cost of the optimal solution in
the last LP provides a lower bound for the MRPP.

7. Conclusions
We have developed a "rst heuristic procedure, based on the resolution of some minimum cost
#ow and matching problems, that obtains, in short computation time, good solutions for the Mixed
Rural Postman Problem. Moreover, we have also presented a local search procedure based on the
Tabu Search methodology, to provide high quality solutions.
Experiments with 270 randomly generated graphs were performed to assess the merits of the
procedures developed here. Our Tabu Search implementation was shown to perform remarkably
well considering the average deviation with respect to a lower bound, obtained with an LP-based
cutting plane procedure, and its running time.
