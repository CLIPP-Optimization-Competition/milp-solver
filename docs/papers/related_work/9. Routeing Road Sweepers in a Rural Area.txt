Routeing Road Sweepers in a Rural Area
RICHARD W. EGLESE and HEATHER MURDOCK
Department of Operational Research and Operations Management, Lancaster University
A computer program has been developed to assist the routeing of road-sweeping vehicles in a part of the
north-west of England. The aim of the study was to develop a method which would specify the route to
be followed by each sweeper which minimized the dead distance travelled, subject to constraints on the
capacity of the sweeper and the time available in any day. The method had to be flexible so that the
effects of changes, such as new vehicles, alterations to the location of tips for detritus, or different fre-
quencies of sweeping could be examined quickly. The paper describes how the design of the heuristic
algorithm was influenced by the geography of the area, the computing facilities and the time available to
complete the study.
Key words: Chinese postman problem, heuristics, local government, microcomputers, routeing
BACKGROUND
Highway authorities have a duty to sweep roads for public safety and for maintenance. This must
be done at a requisite minimum frequency. In addition, the local authority has discretion to sweep
roads more frequently for amenity and public-health reasons. The local authority may carry out
the sweeping using their own men and vehicles, but may agree to have certain areas swept by
other local authorities. Since the introduction of the Local Government Planning and Land Act
1980, the direct-labour organizations employed by the local authorities must demonstrate good
value for money and must compete with outside contractors, by way of tender, for a proportion of
their work loads, which could include road sweeping.
Lancashire County Council found that their manual method of routeing road"sweeping vehicles
was too slow and inflexible to take account of changes which may take place in the road-sweeping
requirements. They therefore asked a team from the Department of Operational Research and
Operations Management at Lancaster University to develop a method for routeing their road-
sweeping vehicles. The new method had to be efficient in that it aimed to minimize the dead
distance travelled (i.e. the roads which had to be traversed, without being swept). It was also
necessary for the method to be sufficiently precise so that the work done could be measured. This
would be needed when bidding for the sweeping of a new area or assessing bids from other
contractors to ensure good value for money. The measurement of the work was also important as
a component of the drivers' bonus payments.
The method used was to be sufficiently flexible to be able to incorporate new roads, new
vehicles with different speeds or capacities and new policies on the frequency of road sweeping,
and to have the ability to incorporate local knowledge. A secondary objective was that manage-
ment preferred a routeing system which ensured that both sides of a road were planned to be
swept in the same day. This would make checking and supervision of the work easier.
These requirements pointed to designing a computer-based tool. In order for it to be used by
area level management,it needed to be capable of running on a PC-compatible machine, several
of which were locally available.
DESCRIPTION OF THE PROBLEM
The county is divided into several districts. Each district is swept by a sweeper based at a local
depot. The sweeper starts from the depot at the start of the day and must return there empty at
the end of the day. The limited capacity of the road-sweeping vehicles means that a sweeper must
be emptied perhaps several times during the day as well as at the end of the day's sweeping. One
feature of the rural situation compared with ·many similar problems in urban areas is that there
are several tipping sites from which to choose to empty the sweeper, which are distributed round
the district. The depot is not generally a tipping site.

The roads in a district are classified according to the frequency with which they are to be swept.
For example, 'A' roads might be swept at least 12 times a year while some rural roads might be
swept only once.
There are two further features of the type of situation examined here which would not necessar-
ily be found in a more urban environment. The first is that there are very few one-way streets in
the area. In the few cases where one-way streets exist, they are normally swept in one direction by
a sweeper going against the traffic taking suitable precautions, so they could be regarded as
two-way streets from the point of view of forming routes. Dual carriageways, where both the
nearside and offside kerbs are to be swept, can be dealt with by simply representing the dual
carriageway as two roads between the same pair of junctions. The second feature is that there are
no additional constraints which imply that certain roads can only be swept at certain times. This
is sometimes the case in urban environments where parking regulations may have a significant
effect on the ability of sweepers to sweep the kerbs and thus require that the roadsides are further
classified according to their availability for sweeping in different time windows; see e.g. Bodin and
Kursh. 1•2
SOLUTION APPROACH
The problem of finding a route of minimum distance which covers all arcs in a network is
known as the. Chinese postman problem. The routeing problem described in the previous section
is a directed Chinese postman problem with constraints on the capacity of the vehicles and the
time available to them in a day. Although there are efficient algorithms available for solving the
directed and undirected Chinese postman problems optimally, the addition of capacity constraints
makes the problems much harder. Golden and Wong3 have shown that the capacitated Chinese
postman problem (CCPP) is NP-hard, which means that it is one of a set of many combinatorial
optimization problems for which no algorithm has yet been devised which will guarantee an
optimal solution in a number of steps which is a polynomial function of the size of the problem.
Other practical routeing problems, such as refuse collection and winter gritting operations, can
also be formulated as a CCPP, but in these examples the vehicles only have to pass down each
road in the network once. These are therefore examples of undirected CCPPs and methods sug-
gested for solving problems of this type (e.g. methods described in Golden et al.4) are not appro-
priate to the road sweeping problem. A discussion of approaches to various arc-routeing problems
is contained in Bodin et al. 5
Therefore it was decided to design a heuristic to produce feasible routes, with as little dead
distance as possible, though without guaranteeing an optimal solution. This is the approach
adopted by Bodin and Kursh. 1 •2 However, Bodin and Kursh's method is designed to cope with an
area containing many one-way streets, as are found in New York. Therefore it was inappropriate
to make use of their method for an area where all roads could be treated as two-way streets.
Treating all roads as pairs of one-way streets would imply extra data storage requirements and
use of Bodin and Kursh's method would not generate routes which implied that both sides of any
road would be swept on the same day. Bodin and Kursh's method also deals with time window
constraints, an unnecessary complication in our application. Indeed, taking advantage of the fact
that all roads could be treated as two-way streets meant that it was possible to devise a simple
algorithm which could easily incorporate user interaction if necessary and would generate routes
which implied that both sides of any road would be swept on the same day.
It was decided to design the heuristic within a visual interactive model so that users could
test the consequences of alternative routeing decisions as well as obtaining results for different
scenarios.
Two research assistants were employed for a year to work on this project plus two other pro-
jects requiring a similar amount of work for the same client. They did not have the benefit of being
able to make use of a digitized road network database, as the County was still in the process of
deciding which geographical information system to use and did not have such a database avail-
able. Therefore it was decided that one district would be selected on which the routeing program
would operate and that part of the project would have to include digitizing the road network in
this district. This was done by creating text files of the co-ordinates of road junctions and bends

The approach to this problem was partly influenced by the limited time available. The particu-
lar heuristic approach adopted was one where we were sure the basic algorithm could be imple-
mented in the time available, but, if time allowed, the heuristic could be refined to reduce the
occasions where the heuristic performed badly. As will be described in the next section, the team
were able to develop the heuristic to an extent where it produced acceptable routes in most
situations. By designing the heuristic within a visual interactive modelling environment, the system
makes it easy for a user to modify the routes produced if he or she wishes to test alternatives. As a
strong visual representation of the problem is available through the map, human abilities can be
used to detect areas of possible improvement which could be difficult to program automatically.
The interactive heuristic approach is discussed more generally and with reference to other exam-
ples in Eglese. 6
SOLUTION METHOD
The problem is made more tractable by decomposing it in two ways: first on the basis of
sweeping frequency and secondly according to the tip which will be used to empty the sweeper.
The roads in a district are classified according to the frequency with which they are to be swept.
The classifications used in the study were:
A-'A' roads,
B-'B' roads,
C-unclassified rural roads,
D-unclassified estate or village roads.

The higher the class of road, the more often it was required to be swept, so the computer
program allows four possible subsets of roads to be selected for a sweeping schedule:
1-A roads only,
2-A and B roads only,
3-A, Band C roads only,
4-A, B, C and D roads.
Then if, for example, A roads are to be swept 12 times in a year and Broads 8 times a year then
the A road schedule (option 1) can be used 4 times and the A and B road schedule (option 2) can
be used 8 times in a year. This decomposition allows changes to the frequency of sweeping differ-
ent classes of roads to be made easily.' In the example given, the proposed method implies that B
roads will be swept irregularly, sometimes every month and sometimes every other month. If this
had been unacceptable, it would have been necessary to prepare routes which swept B roads only.
Each road in the district is also classified according to the nearest tip. The program develops
routes for each tip area separately, and so ensures that the nearest tip is always used when the
sweeper must be emptied during the course of a day. Tests were carried out to see whether any
improvement could be gained by splitting up the district, so that each tip area consisted of the
roads for which the distance to the tip plus the distance to the depot was minimized, but this
strategy increased the dead distance travelled.
The routeing algorithm is based on the observation that given a set of two-way roads which
must be swept each side and no capacity constraints, a route may be constructed to sweep both
sides of each road without incurring any dead distance by simply taking the next unswept road at
each junction and returning down the other side of the present road if all other roads from the
junction have been swept on one or both sides (see Figure 2). In urban areas this method of
routeing may require modification if there are problems in carrying out the U-turns implied.
However, in the predominantly rural setting of this study no modifications were needed to reduce
the number of U-turns.

The algorithm proceeds from one junction to a neighbouring junction at each iteration. As each
junction is reached, a record is kept of the distance swept since the sweeper was last emptied and
the time elapsed since the start of the day. A test is applied to see whether the distance swept since
the sweeper was last emptied plus the distance to be swept down roads which have had only one
side swept is greater than the distance which the sweeper can sweep before emptying. If this is so,
the time available for sweeping is reduced by an estimate of the time required to go to the nearest

tip, empty the vehicle and return to continue sweeping. A second test calculates whether the time
remaining in the day is enough to enable the sweeper to sweep the road being considered, finish
sweeping the other sides of roads swept once, and finally empty and return to the depot. When the
sweeper finally empties at the end of the day, it is routed to go to the tip for which the distance to
the tip from the end of sweeping plus the distance from the tip to the depot is minimized. If neither
test is positive, the algorithm then normally proceeds by selecting the next unswept road on the
left as the road to be next swept, returning down the other side of the last road if no unswept road
is available.
This method of producing routes has the advantage from a supervisory point of view that when
a road is included in the route for the day, both sides of the road are due to be swept in the same
day.
The first route constructed in a tip area is started at the junction nearest to the depot, though
this may be overridden to any other junction if required. Each new day's route starts as close as
possible to where the last day's route finished. This means that if a route is not completed one day
it could be completed on the next day and the next day's route started without a long dead
distance in between.
If the route returns to the starting junction and there are no more unswept roads off it, but
there is still time remaining in the day, the algorithm will search for the nearest junction from
which to continue sweeping. This is required, for example, when the roads to be swept form
disconnected sets. Dijkstra's algorithm is used whenever shortest distances and routes are
required. Smith7 provides a suitable Pascal code.

(c) Some roads could be left isolated and unswept after the sweeping route had passed through

In the proposed method for selecting the next road at a junction, no account is taken of how far
the tip will be from the sweeper when the sweeper next needs emptying. It was decided not to
attempt to take this into account because there could be variability in the distance swept before
the sweeper was full, depending on the weather conditions when sweeping and the state of the
roads. A routeing method which relied on the stability of the capacity constraint for its effec-
tiveness would often be found to be impractical. If the distance swept before the sweeper was full
were more consistent, it would have been possible to modify the proposed algorithm so that the
capacity constraint was treated in a similar way to the time constraint. The sweeper would then
start and therefore finish each section before emptying as close as possible to each tip.
Of course, the variability in the distance swept before the sweeper is full is a problem for any
method used to plan the work. Some of the variability can be anticipated on a particular day; for
example when wet dead leaves must be swept up the sweeper will become full relatively quickly.
However, there is still much variability which cannot be anticipated in advance. Nevertheless, the
computer system enables the sensitivity of the workload to this variability to be explored. The
sweeping speed and speed covering dead distance are both relatively stable parameters, but the
consequences of variations can be examined using the computer program.
USING THE COMPUTER PROGRAM
The program was written in Turbo Pascal and designed to run on a PC-compatible with a
colour monitor and graphics capability.
The database is first read in and the user specifies which set of roads is required to be swept,
which tip area is to be used, and from which junction the first route is to start. Before routeing

commences, the user has the opportunity of altering the following parameters from their initial
default values:
the sweeping speed;
the dead travel speed;
the length of day;
the capacity of the sweeper (in terms of maximum distance before emptying);
the tip area to which a road is assigned;
the class of a road;
the priority of selecting a particular road for sweeping when constructing the routes.
The visual display shows a map of the tip· area and the roads to be swept. As a road is swept on
one side its colour is changed and when it has been swept on the other side the colour is changed
again. The time spent sweeping, travelling over dead distance and in total are shown on the
screen, as are the corresponding distances. A message indicates when and where the sweeper is full
and must go to the tip to empty. There is also a facility to 'zoom' so that part of the area can be
seen in greater detail. The user may also opt to use the manual override while the route is being
constructed. In this mode, the road which the program would select as being the next to be swept
is flashed on the screen and the user may accept or reject it. If it is rejected and a different road
could be selected then the alternative roads are successively flashed until one is accepted.
A text file is created by the program which contains the details of the routes constructed and
gives the total distances and times required. This file provides the basic information required to
measure the work involved in sweeping an area and it may also be used to produce maps showing
the different routes. The file can also be used to produce a written explanation of the routes for the
driver of the sweeping vehicle. An example of this form of output is shown in Figure 5.
RESULTS AND CONCLUSIONS
When the computer program had been applied to the district under study which was swept by
one sweeper, the routes formed implied that the sweeper could complete the sweeping required
with the specified frequencies over a year in a total of 62 working days. On average the sweeper
had to be emptied twice during each day's route plus once at the end of the day. The solution
required some roads to be reassigned to different tip areas and in a few cases the automatic route
selection was overridden. It was difficult to make a precise comparison between the splution
generated using the computer program and current practice. This was because road sweepi'ng had
been managed in the past on a broad basis without the need for measurement. The sweeper was
also used for additional work such as sweeping accident debris or attendance on surface dressing
operations. However, the impression was that about half of the sweeper's working time was spent
on routine sweeping. The result then clearly represented a reduction in the number of days that
were estimated to be used by the sweeper at present on routine sweeping.
In practice some of the work additional to routine sweeping may take part of a day, leaving the
rest for routine sweeping. The computer program provides a flexible tool which enables routes to
be set up taking a shorter time than a full day if that is what is required.
Local management have used the program to produce routes for the district, sometimes adjust-
ing the parameters used and have found the program easy to use. The program can be run
without any user intervention to give routes for a set of roads in a district within a few minutes.
The driver's instructions for some of the routes produced were taken and tested out in practice.
The instructions were found to be clear to follow.
The County has now obtained a geographic information system and is well on the way to
completing the digitization of the complete road network. Since the project was completed the
County's programmers have written an interface which allows the roads in any area of the
County to be selected and the information passed to a PC in a form which will input to the
sweeping programme.
The software provided has been accepted as a useful tool for the management of the road-
sweeping operation. The program ensures that feasible and efficient routes are produced, with the
computer taking care of all the calculations of distances covered and time spent. The amount of
manual intervention required to produce good routes is minimal. The program is flexible and easy
